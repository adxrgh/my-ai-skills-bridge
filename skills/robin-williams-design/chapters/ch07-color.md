# Chapter 7: 颜色运用 (Color)

## Core Idea
The color wheel is a simple, powerful tool for choosing coordinated color schemes with confidence. This chapter explains the color wheel relationships, dark/light variations (shades/tints), warm/cool color behavior, and the CMYK vs RGB color models for print vs. screen.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Color Wheel**:
  - **Primary colors (三原色)**: Yellow, Red, Blue — cannot be created by mixing.
  - **Secondary colors (三间色)**: Green, Purple, Orange — equal mixtures of adjacent primaries.
  - **Tertiary colors (第三色)**: Yellow-orange, blue-green, etc. — equal mixtures of adjacent primary+secondary, filling the wheel to 12 colors.
- **Color Relationships (schemes)**:
  - **Complementary (互补)**: Colors opposite each other on the wheel. Best used with one color dominant and the other as accent.
  - **Triad (三色组)**: Three colors equally spaced — e.g., red/yellow/blue ("primary triad," classic for children's products); green/orange/purple ("secondary triad"). Other triads share a connecting base color, so they look harmonious.
  - **Split-complementary (分裂互补三色组)**: Pick a color, find its complement, use the two colors on either side of the complement instead. Softer, more refined edges.
  - **Analogous (类似色)**: Adjacent colors on the wheel; share a base color → coordinated. Combining different tints/shades of an analogous set is striking.
  - **Monochromatic (单色)**: One hue plus its tints and shades — always harmonious (like black-and-white photos).
  - **Tints & Shades combos**: Use any of the above relationships with tints/shades instead of pure hues — greatly expands choices while staying safe (e.g., dark reds + dark greens avoid the "Christmas" cliché; muted primary triads escape the "children's toy" feel).

## Key Concepts
- **Hue / Tint / Shade (色调/亮色/暗色)**: Pure color = hue; add white = tint (亮色); add black = shade (暗色).
- **Wildflower Theory of Color (野花理论)**: Any combination of colors found in nature looks fine together — the only problem is when *values* (色质) are too close, causing visual vibration/blurriness. If similar values must be used, keep them apart and use unequal amounts.
- **Warm vs. Cool (暖色与冷色)**: Warm colors contain red/yellow (advancing — grab attention instantly); cool colors contain blue (receding — work as backgrounds). **Use less warm color than cool**; never balance them 50/50. The eye goes to warm first — control what the reader sees first.
- **How to choose colors (如何取舍)**:
  - Seasonal project? Use analogous colors suggesting the season (hot reds/yellows = summer; icy blues = winter; oranges/browns = autumn; bright greens = spring).
  - Company official colors? Start there, add tints/shades.
  - Logo with specific colors? Use a split-complementary of them.
  - Photo/image present? Pull a color from the photo (eyedropper), build an analogous (calm) or complementary (energetic) palette around it.
  - Recurring project? Build a consistent palette used across all issues; choose the palette BEFORE starting a multi-part project.

## Mental Models
- **"The color wheel makes you confident"**: Weird combinations you'd fear are actually coordinated — the wheel lets you use them boldly.
- **"Warm advances, cool recedes"**: A practical perceptual rule: the reader's eye goes to warm colors first.
- **"Colors affect everything around them"**: A color influences all neighboring objects — context matters.

## Anti-patterns
- **Values too close together** — causes visual vibration and unreadable contrast.
- **Equal amounts of warm and cool** — uncomfortably strong; use less warm.
- **Complementary red+green at full saturation** — instantly reads "Christmas."
- **Primary triad at full saturation** — instantly reads "children's toy."

## Reference Tables
- **CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black)**: 4-color process printing — for anything printed on a physical medium (books, magazines, posters). Mixing works like paint (blue+yellow=green).
- **RGB (Red, Green, Blue)**: Light-based model for screens (monitors, TV, phones). Mixing red+green=yellow (light mixes differently); all colors = white; none = black.
- **Rule of thumb**: print → CMYK; screen → RGB. Work in RGB for image editing (smaller files, more Photoshop tools), convert to CMYK at the end (each back-and-forth conversion loses data). Screen colors always look richer than print — expect the loss.

## Worked Example
**Postcard in monochrome**: Only tints of black printed — looks multi-toned; another version printed in tints/shades of one purple. Restricting yourself to a monochrome palette is a great practice exercise.

**Shakespeare booklet series**: Six tertiary colors at 80% tint, one per cover, cycled over the years — a deliberate, organized color-coding system for a recurring publication.

**Ball & worm illustration**: Colors sampled from the image (eyedropper in InDesign) used for title and subtitles; camel-colored background tones for the rest; bright wing color for accents.

## Key Takeaways
1. The color wheel (12 colors) is the confidence tool for color choice.
2. Five reliable schemes: complementary, triad, split-complementary, analogous, monochromatic — plus tints/shades versions of each.
3. Hue/tint/shade = pure color / +white / +black.
4. Keep color *values* (明暗) distinct to avoid vibration.
5. Warm advances, cool recedes — use less warm, more cool.
6. Print = CMYK, screen = RGB; edit in RGB, convert to CMYK for print.
7. Choose the palette before starting a multi-part project; use the eyedropper to pull colors from photos.
