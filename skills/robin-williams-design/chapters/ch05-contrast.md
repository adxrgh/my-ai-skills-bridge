# Chapter 5: 对比 (Contrast)

## Core Idea
Contrast is the most effective way to add visual interest to a page and to create an organized hierarchy among elements. The golden rule: contrast must be STRONG. If two elements are not the same, make them *very* different. Never be a wimp.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Contrast Principle**: Elements on the page should contrast with one another to attract the reader's eye. If two items are not exactly the same, make them distinctly different.
  - When: Always — it's usually the most important factor that draws the reader to a page first.
  - How: Contrast via font size, font style, line weight, color (warm/cool), texture (smooth/rough), shape (horizontal vs. vertical elements), spacing (wide vs. tight line spacing), image size, etc.
- **Contrast vs. Conflict**: If two elements are different but not different *enough*, that's not contrast — that's conflict. 12pt vs 14pt type, 0.5pt vs 1pt lines, dark brown vs black: all conflicts, not contrasts. When in doubt, make it MORE different.

## Key Concepts
- **Contrast organizes information**: It's not just decoration — contrast creates hierarchy, guides the reader through the page, and establishes focus. Readers understand the document's structure at a glance.
- **Combining with repetition**: Contrast + repetition are natural partners. E.g., a masthead set in the same bold face as the headings (repetition), but the masthead much bigger (contrast). A black band under the title can be repeated elsewhere in black.
- **Resume fix pattern**: A typical resume becomes instantly scannable with: one alignment (left), bold section headers, larger space between paragraphs than within, bold degree/job titles.
- **Beyond type**: Contrast can come from lines (2pt vs 4pt rules — never 0.5 vs 1pt), colors (with real contrast to black body text), spacing, texture.

## Mental Models
- **"Never wimp out"**: The #1 mental model of the whole book. Timid differences read as mistakes; strong differences read as intent.
- **"If two things aren't the same, make them very different"**: The decisive test for every pair of elements.
- **"Contrast is the eye-catcher"**: If a page doesn't grab the eye, nobody reads it, no matter how good the content.
- **Design principles are interrelated**: Contrast usually appears with the other three principles; they work as a system.

## Anti-patterns
- **Weak differences** — 12 vs 14pt fonts, 0.5 vs 1pt lines, dark brown vs black text.
- **Too many similar-weight elements** competing for attention.
- **Contrast only in type** — forgetting lines, colors, spacing, texture, and shape.
- **Repeating elements so heavily that contrast is lost** (over-repetition muddies emphasis).

## Worked Example
**Two identical newsletters**: Same layout, same info. Version 1 is clean but bland — nothing grabs the eye. Version 2: bold font for title and subtitles, title changed from ALL-CAPS to mixed case (allowing a larger, bolder face), black band under the title repeated elsewhere. Version 2 wins attention immediately — and that's the whole point.

**Resume before**: Job title unclear, mixed center+left alignment, equal spacing everywhere, dates sometimes left sometimes right. **After**: single left alignment, bold headers (immediate scan), paragraph spacing > line spacing, bold degree/job titles (repetition of the heading font). The document's purpose and organization become instantly visible.

**Table with lines**: Fine 0.5pt and 1pt rules look like mistakes. Switching to clearly different weights (thin + 2/4pt thick) makes the design lively and clearly intentional; a thick rule behind white text creates a striking band.

## Key Takeaways
1. Contrast is the single most effective way to add visual interest and hierarchy.
2. If elements differ, make them *obviously* different — timid contrast reads as error.
3. Contrast organizes information and guides reading order — not just decoration.
4. Use type, line weight, color, spacing, texture, and shape for contrast.
5. Pair contrast with repetition for unified, lively layouts.
6. Strong contrast creates a focal point that pulls the reader in.
