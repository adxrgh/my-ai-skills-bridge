# Chapter 11: 字体类别 (Type Categories)

## Core Idea
Most of the thousands of typefaces fall into six categories: **Oldstyle (旧式体), Modern (现代体), Slab serif (粗衬线体), Sans serif (无衬线体), Script (手写体), Decorative (花体)**. Before contrasting fonts deliberately, you must understand the *similarities* within each category — similarity is what causes conflict when fonts are combined.

## Frameworks Introduced
### The Six Categories (with identification features):
1. **Oldstyle (旧式体)** — based on the hand-written letter with a V-nib pen:
   - Serifs (with brackets) on lowercase letters, always at an angle (the pen angle).
   - Moderate thick/thin transition (stroke contrast) in curved strokes.
   - **Diagonal stress** (imaginary line through the thinnest parts of curves is diagonal).
   - "Invisible" — nothing distracts from reading → **best choice for large amounts of body text**.
2. **Modern (现代体)** — 18th-century mechanical era:
   - Serifs are **thin and horizontal** (not slanted); **vertical stress**; severe thick/thin transition.
   - Cold, elegant look; striking at large sizes.
   - Poor for body text — thin strokes nearly vanish, thick ones dominate ("glare" on the page).
3. **Slab serif (粗衬线体)** — born with advertising/industrial revolution:
   - Little to no thick/thin transition; heavy uniform strokes.
   - Also called **Clarendon** (the archetype) or **Egyptian** (Memphis, Cairo, Scarab — Egyptian names).
   - Good readability for large text (Clarendon, New Century Schoolbook); pages look darker than Oldstyle; clean, direct — common in children's books.
4. **Sans serif (无衬线体)** — "sans" = without:
   - Almost always **monoweight** (no visible thick/thin transition).
   - Widely accepted only since the early 20th century.
   - **Warning**: Helvetica/Arial and Verdana are screen-designed — not ideal for print. Introduce a sans family with a wide weight range (very light to very black) for real design power.
   - Rare exceptions with slight transition (e.g., **Optima**) are hard to combine — they share stroke similarity with serifs yet lack serifs; use with care.
5. **Script (手写体)** — handwritten with calligraphy pen/brush:
   - Like cheesecake: **use sparingly or it's nauseating**. Never set long passages; NEVER in ALL CAPS. But large-size script can be very impressive — don't be conservative.
6. **Decorative (花体)** — instantly recognizable; if a whole book in this face makes you want to throw it, it's decorative:
   - Fun, special, easy, often cheap — but usage must be **limited**.
   - Try the counter-intuitive trick: if a face strikes you as informal, test it in a formal context (and vice versa) — context changes its meaning entirely.
   - Great for quotes.

## Key Concepts
- **Stress (强调线)**: the imaginary line through the thinnest parts of curved strokes — diagonal in Oldstyle, vertical in Modern.
- **Thick/thin transition (粗/细过渡)**: moderate (Oldstyle), severe (Modern), none/near-none (Slab, Sans).
- **x-height (x字母高度)**: height of the lowercase x — long in most sans; unusually short in some faces (e.g., Bernhard) with very tall ascenders. Details like this matter for pairing.
- **Extended vs. condensed (扩展 vs. 压缩)**: expanded faces look spread out; condensed look squeezed — each has its place.

## Mental Models
- **"See type as clearly as possible"**: train your eye by classifying fonts everywhere (magazines, books, packaging) — even imperfect classification sharpens perception.
- **"Similarity within a category = conflict source"**: the reason to study categories is to avoid accidental near-matches when combining fonts.
- **"Script is cheesecake; decorative is a special occasion"**: both are high-impact, low-tolerance — use tiny amounts.

## Anti-patterns
- Setting body text in Modern type (glare, vanishing thin strokes).
- Long text or ALL CAPS in Script faces.
- Whole designs (or books) in Decorative faces.
- Relying only on Helvetica/Arial/Verdana for sans (screen fonts, weak for print).
- Combining Optima-style "hybrid" sans carelessly.

## Worked Example
**Optima + FunnyBone**: Optima's Oldstyle elegance (despite being sans) contrasts beautifully with FunnyBone's energy — but it needs extra contrast (size/weight/color) to fully click.

## Key Takeaways
1. Six categories: Oldstyle, Modern, Slab, Sans, Script, Decorative.
2. Identify by serifs, stress (diagonal/vertical), and thick/thin transition.
3. Oldstyle = best body text; Modern = large display; Slab = heavy display/children's books; Sans = get a family with real weight range; Script/Decorative = tiny amounts.
4. Train your eye: classify fonts in the wild; build sample collections and subsets (by x-height, ascender length, extended/condensed).
5. Understanding similarities is the prerequisite for deliberate contrast (Ch 12).
