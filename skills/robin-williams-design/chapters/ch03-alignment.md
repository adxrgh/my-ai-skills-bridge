# Chapter 3: 对齐 (Alignment)

## Core Idea
Nothing should be placed on a page arbitrarily. Every element should have a visual connection with another element on the page. Alignment creates invisible lines that bind elements together, producing a unified, cohesive, and refined appearance — even when elements are physically far apart.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Alignment Principle**: Every item should align with at least one other item. Find a strong, clear alignment line and stick to it.
  - When: Any layout with text blocks, images, or mixed elements.
  - How: Align text baselines horizontally; align text block edges (left or right); align image edges with page/other element edges.
- **Strong vs. Weak Edges**: Left-aligned and right-aligned text create a "hard" vertical edge — a strong, visible alignment line. Centered text has only a "soft" center line, which is weak and does not unify the layout as powerfully.
- **One Alignment Per Page**: Until you are trained, use only ONE alignment style per page (all left, all right, or all centered). Mixing alignments creates chaos unless the blocks share a boundary line.
- **Breaking the Rules Deliberately**: Once alignment is strong and obvious, you may deliberately break it — but it must look intentional. "Before you break the rules, you must know what the rules are" (Robin's rule).

## Key Concepts
- **Invisible alignment line**: The imagined line connecting aligned elements; its strength gives the layout force.
- **Centered = safe but boring**: Centered alignment is the beginner's default, feels safe, looks formal/stable — but usually dull. Avoid it as a default; choose it deliberately for formal occasions (e.g., wedding invitations).
- **First paragraph is never indented**: Indentation signals a new paragraph, but the first paragraph is obviously new. Standard typeset indent = 1 em (about 2 spaces), not 5 spaces/"typewriter" indents.
- **Trapped whitespace**: Ugly-shaped blank space between a photo and text that visually separates related elements. Fix by moving the photo so its edge lines up with the text edge, letting the whitespace drift to one side.
- **Do not center a headline over left-aligned body text**: The reader can't tell the headline is centered; it looks like it's just hanging there.

## Mental Models
- **"Find the strongest line"**: Look for a clear edge to align to — a photo's straight edge, a text block's left edge, a rule, or a page margin — and base everything on it.
- **"The eye wants order"**: Our eyes are drawn to ordered arrangements; order feels calm and safe. Sloppy alignment reads as amateur.
- **"Say the problem out loud"**: Describing alignment problems in words (e.g., "this headline is centered over ragged-left text") is the path to fixing them.
- **Alignment is stronger than proximity**: Alignment tells readers that items belong to the same group even when they are physically far apart.

## Anti-patterns
- **Centering as the default** — the beginner's crutch; produces formal, dull layouts.
- **Mixing alignment styles** on one page (center + left + right) without a shared edge.
- **Aligning text blocks but leaving images unaligned** (e.g., captions centered under photos).
- **Centering a headline over indented/left-aligned text.**
- **Wimpy rule-breaking** — breaking alignment slightly so it looks like a mistake, not a choice.
- **Tiny, weak differences** — using 12pt vs 14pt fonts or 0.5pt vs 1pt lines creates conflict, not contrast.

## Worked Example
**Business card before**: 3 groups with 3 different alignments (left, right, center), elements not on shared baselines or edges. Everything looks randomly tossed onto the card.

**After — all elements aligned to the right edge**: The information immediately becomes organized. A strong right edge is established; related items are also grouped with proximity. The invisible boundary line connects all the text blocks.

**Report cover before**: Centered, everything floating, standard but boring and amateur-looking.

**After — left-aligned**: Title and author's name far apart but connected by the strong left alignment line. The cover looks more refined and professional.

**Tri-State Wellness ad**: All cells were centered but not aligned to each other; whitespace was scattered. Redesign: align columns, put phone numbers at top of each column, align text edges — the page gains breathing room, the logo grows, and a blue band (repetition) anchors the bottom.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find a clear alignment line and base every element on it.
2. Left/right alignment beats centering for creating strong, unified layouts.
3. Never center a headline over left-aligned text.
4. Mixing alignment styles is only OK when blocks share a visible boundary.
5. Deliberate rule-breaking works only when the rest of the layout is clearly aligned.
6. Check for trapped whitespace between photos and text.
