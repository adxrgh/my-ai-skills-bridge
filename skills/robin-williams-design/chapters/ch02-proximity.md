# Chapter 2: 亲密性 (Proximity)

## Core Idea
Related items should be physically grouped together to form visual units. Unrelated items should be separated with more space. This creates instant organization, reduces clutter, and tells the reader where to start, where to go next, and when they're done.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Proximity Principle**: If elements are related in meaning, they should be close in space. If they are unrelated, they should not be close.
  - When to use: Anytime you have multiple pieces of information on a page — business cards, flyers, newsletters, menus, reports.
  - How: Group related items; increase space between unrelated groups; use consistent spacing within groups.
- **The "Squint Test" for Proximity**: Squint at the page and count how many times your eye stops. If there are more than 3–5 visual stops, you likely have too many isolated elements that need grouping.
- **Whitespace as Relationship**: The amount of space between elements directly communicates whether they are related or separate. Equal spacing between all elements implies everything is equally related (which is usually wrong).

## Key Concepts
- **Visual unit**: A group of items perceived as one entity because of their spatial closeness.
- **Trapped whitespace**: Blank space that sits between unrelated elements, visually splitting them apart incorrectly. Avoid trapping whitespace between elements that belong together.
- **Paragraph spacing control**: Use software paragraph spacing (before/after) instead of hitting Enter twice. This gives precise, repeatable control over group spacing.

## Mental Models
- **"Space speaks before words do"**: Readers perceive spatial relationships before they read any text. The layout tells them the information architecture instantly.
- **"Group first, align second, then add contrast"**: Proximity is usually the first principle to apply when organizing information. Get the groups right before refining alignment or contrast.
- **"Same space = same group"**: If two elements have the same distance from a third element, readers will assume all three belong together. Be deliberate about which elements are closer.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equal spacing everywhere**: When every element is equally spaced, nothing stands out as related. The page becomes a chaotic collection of isolated fragments.
- **Corner-filling**: Placing elements in corners just because there's empty space, regardless of relationships.
- **Overcrowding**: Filling every inch of space because you're afraid of blank space. White space is not wasted space — it's an active design element.
- **Mixed relationships hidden by proximity**: Sometimes items are placed close together that shouldn't be related (e.g., a title equidistant from the paragraph above and below it, making it unclear which text it belongs to).

## Worked Example
**Before — Chaotic Business Card**: Name, title, company, address, phone, email — all scattered with random spacing. The eye stops 5+ times with no clear reading path.

**After — Organized Business Card**: Group related info:
- Top-left block: Name (bold, larger) + Title (underneath, close)
- Right block: Company name
- Bottom block: Address / Phone / Email grouped tightly
- Space between the three blocks clearly separates them

Result: The eye knows exactly where to start, follows a clear path, and understands the hierarchy without reading a word.

**Menu Example**: A menu with all dishes and descriptions in one undifferentiated block. Applying proximity:
- Group appetizers together with tight spacing
- Larger space before entrees group
- Prices aligned near (but clearly separate from) descriptions
- Section headers closer to their section than to other sections

Result: Customers can scan the menu quickly and understand the structure at a glance.

## Key Takeaways
1. Proximity creates organization automatically — just by grouping related items.
2. If information is logically related, it must be visually related through closeness.
3. Unrelated items need deliberate separation — don't let them drift together.
4. Use software paragraph spacing, not double Enter, to control group distances precisely.
5. Squint at your design: if your eye stops more than 3–5 times on a simple page, regroup.
6. White space is not empty space — it's a tool that defines relationships.
