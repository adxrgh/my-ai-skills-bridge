# Appendix 1: A Worked Example - An Indian Village

## Core Idea
The village study demonstrates the full path from a broad, culturally situated inventory of misfits through interaction analysis and hierarchical decomposition to constructive diagrams and physical components.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-source requirement inventory**
  1. Needs explicitly felt by villagers.
  2. Needs arising from regional or national economic and social purposes.
  3. Conditions already satisfied implicitly and therefore easy to overlook.
- **Category labels are staging aids only**
  - Initial headings help collection but must not control decomposition.
- **Set-to-diagram translation**
  - Read each derived set for shared physical implications; construct a spatial unit that resolves several variables together.

## Key Concepts
- **Set `M`**: 141 village misfit variables.
- **Initial categories**: religion and caste, social forces, agriculture, animal husbandry, employment, water, welfare, education, health, implementation, and regional development.
- **Compound**: a physical/social unit for several families.
- **Constructive diagram**: a minimal physical relation answering one derived set.

## Mental Models
- Include invisible successes in the brief; redesign can accidentally destroy them.
- A socially contested function may require a distributed or linear center rather than a geometrically central building.
- A strange cluster of variables may share a strong spatial implication despite weak verbal similarity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Exported modernization checklist**: ignores lived needs and implicit functioning.
- **Copying collection categories into architecture**: turns headings into buildings without analyzing interactions.
- **Centrality equals center point**: places shared facilities where factional ownership makes them unusable.
- **Diagram as illustration**: draws the solution after the fact instead of using the set to generate a physical relation.

## Worked Example
One derived set combines caste and family grouping, security for women and children, access to water, cottage industry, reduced overcrowding, aid for vulnerable people, and school attendance. The constructive response is a walled compound for roughly 5-10 families. Its wall doubles as a water channel; protected common space supports women's activity and cottage work; the entrance locates work along daily paths; outward expansion is easier than within sealed individual houses. One component answers interacting social, hydraulic, safety, and growth forces.

A second set combines gossip, washing, men's groups, shade, breeze, child supervision, sleeping outdoors, sacred water and trees, pedestrian movement, and school quiet. It suggests water, trees, washing, movement, and sitting as one compound-edge unit rather than separate facilities.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build `M` from insiders, external obligations, and implicit successes.
2. Use topical headings only for collection.
3. Let derived sets provoke new multi-function components.
4. Explain each physical move by the variables it resolves.
5. Treat the example's period assumptions as historical source material, not present-day social truth.
