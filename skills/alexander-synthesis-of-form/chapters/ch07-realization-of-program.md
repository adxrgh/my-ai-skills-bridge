# Chapter 7: The Realization of the Program

## Core Idea
The hierarchy controls the sequence and scale of attention: solve coherent low-level requirement sets as diagrams, then combine them upward. The hierarchy coordinates intuition; it does not mechanically generate the finished form.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hierarchical realization**
  - When to use: translating an analyzed problem into physical structure.
  - How: begin with small terminal sets; create a diagram for each; combine sibling diagrams at the next level; revise where their physical implications conflict; continue upward.
- **Constructive diagram**
  - A diagram must be both a **requirement diagram** (only the relevant functional relations) and a **form diagram** (enough physical content to satisfy them, without arbitrary detail).
- **Alternation of analysis and synthesis**
  - The program focuses attention; intuitive physical invention answers each focused set; higher-level combination tests whether local answers form a coherent whole.

## Key Concepts
- **Realization**: turning the program's functional hierarchy into a physical hierarchy.
- **Diagram**: a physical idea stripped to the features justified by a requirement set.
- **Lower-level set**: a small, tightly coupled subproblem.
- **Composition**: combining diagrams while preserving their functional integrity.
- **Arbitrary characteristic**: a formal commitment not demanded by the active requirements.

## Mental Models
- Work bottom-up for discovery and top-down for integration.
- Treat each node in the tree as a temporary lens, not an autonomous product team.
- Delay decorative or brand-level commitments until the structural implications are stable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mechanical optimization fantasy**: assumes the tree outputs a form without judgment.
- **Independent subdesigns**: solves branches in isolation and bolts them together.
- **Diagram overload**: adds details unrelated to the active variable set.
- **Premature naming**: labels a surprising set as acoustics, circulation, or another familiar category and thereby erases its new physical implication.

## Worked Example
Suppose a requirement set brings together pouring, filling, boil warning, cleaning access, lid safety, and simple manufacture. A conventional vocabulary splits these across spout, opening, whistle, and lid. Seen together, they suggest a single wide, short spout that fills, pours, whistles, and removes the unsafe lid. The unexpected component emerges from the set's shared physical implication.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make one constructive diagram per coherent requirement set.
2. Include everything the set calls for and nothing arbitrary.
3. Combine diagrams recursively, revising at their interfaces.
4. Let physical components emerge from functional clusters.
