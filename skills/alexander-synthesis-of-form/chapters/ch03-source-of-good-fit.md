# Chapter 3: The Source of Good Fit

## Core Idea
Good fit is produced by a process whose corrections can keep pace with the interdependence of the system. Adaptation becomes tractable when the problem contains semi-independent subsystems that can reach equilibrium without constantly disturbing one another.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Error-correcting adaptation**
  - When to use: evolving a form through feedback.
  - How: detect a misfit, change the form locally, observe new effects, and retain corrections that reduce active misfit.
- **Subsystem decomposition**
  - When to use: the time to reach fit explodes because corrections reactivate failures elsewhere.
  - How: find clusters with strong internal interaction and weak external interaction; adapt clusters partly independently; coordinate them at their boundaries.
  - Why it works: adaptation time is governed by coupling, not merely by the number of variables.
- **Misfit reaction time**
  - A process succeeds only if the rate of correction is adequate relative to contextual change and cross-effect propagation.

## Key Concepts
- **Equilibrium**: a state in which active misfits have ceased or remain within tolerable bounds.
- **Homeostasis**: maintaining fit through continuing feedback and correction.
- **Interdependence**: a change in one variable affects the state of others.
- **Subsystem**: a cluster that is internally coherent and comparatively independent of other clusters.
- **Adaptation time**: expected time required for the system to reach fit.

## Mental Models
- Complexity is not item count; it is the pattern of connections among items.
- A useful module is not an arbitrary category but a region in which most consequences remain local.
- If every correction wakes every other problem, the system will adapt by astronomical coincidence rather than directed work.

## Anti-patterns
- **Single-concept clarity**: optimizes one coherent idea while sacrificing other forces.
- **Piecemeal patching**: meets each demand with peripheral devices but never creates total physical organization.
- **Category decomposition**: groups by familiar labels rather than actual interaction.

## Worked Example
Imagine 100 lights, each switching between on (misfit) and off (fit). With no coupling, all turn off quickly. With universal coupling, any lit lamp reactivates the rest, so simultaneous fit is fantastically unlikely. If the lights form ten strongly coupled groups that are weakly coupled to one another, each ten-light group can settle independently; the whole adapts in roughly the time one group needs. The connection pattern changes an impossible task into a manageable one.

## Key Takeaways
1. Map interactions before estimating difficulty.
2. Seek semi-independent clusters, not equal-sized partitions.
3. Design feedback so local repair does not repeatedly destroy global progress.
4. Treat modularity as an empirical property of coupling.
