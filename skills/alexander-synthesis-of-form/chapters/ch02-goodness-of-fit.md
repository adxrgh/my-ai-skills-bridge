# Chapter 2: Goodness of Fit

## Core Idea
Design concerns an ensemble of form and context. Because complex good fit is hard to state positively, represent it through specific potential misfits that can be observed, discussed, and progressively eliminated.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Form-context ensemble**
  - When to use: whenever evaluating a designed artifact, service, environment, or organization.
  - How: name the controllable part as form; name everything making demands on it as context; evaluate relations across their boundary.
- **Fit through misfit variables**
  - How: list concrete ways the ensemble can fail; encode each as a binary variable `x_i`, with `1 = misfit` and `0 = no misfit`; seek a state in which all relevant variables are `0`.
  - Why it works: failures are more specific and perceptible than a total positive description of harmony.
- **Negative specification**
  - Prefer statements that identify an observable failure over vague positive aspirations.

## Key Concepts
- **Ensemble**: the form and its context considered together.
- **Form**: the part of the ensemble under the designer's control.
- **Context**: the part that makes demands on the form.
- **Fit**: mutual acceptability or frictionless coexistence between form and context.
- **Misfit**: one identifiable failure in the relation between form and context.
- **Field description**: a unitary account of contextual forces that would also imply the fitting form; rarely available in real design.

## Mental Models
- Look for the ink marks: as high spots reveal an uneven metal face, concrete failures reveal where the ensemble does not fit.
- Treat good fit as the limiting case left after salient incongruities have been neutralized.
- A requirement belongs neither to form nor context alone; it describes a relation between them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Positive aspiration lists**: terms like delightful, elegant, or flexible do not say what would count as failure.
- **Infinite specification**: trying to enumerate every property of a good solution is impossible without a field description.
- **Artifact-only evaluation**: ignores the context in which the artifact must work.

## Worked Example
To match a button, people reject candidates with visible discrepancies - too large, dark, or many-holed - until none remains. They do not enumerate every positive property of a matching button. Likewise, a house's fit is made operational through failures such as rain entry, dangerous child play, no parking, poor cleaning, or lack of privacy. Each failure becomes a variable describing the ensemble.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the boundary between what can be changed and what makes demands.
2. Turn vague requirements into observable potential failures.
3. Select the finite set of failures most likely to matter; acknowledge that it is provisional.
4. Evaluate the ensemble, not the form in isolation.
